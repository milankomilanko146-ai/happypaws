Why the Scale Stops Moving After Week 3 (and What to Change When It Does)

It usually isn't willpower, and it isn't your metabolism being "broken." Here is what is actually going on.

By the Steady Plan team · 8 min read
IMAGE: person standing on a scale, thoughtful expression, natural light

Picture week one of a new diet.

You are motivated. You cut back, you stay on track, and the scale drops. By the second week your clothes feel looser and you are telling people about it.

Then, around week three, the number stops moving.

You did not slip. You are eating the same meals and putting in the same effort, maybe more. But the scale sits there. So you cut a little more. You skip a snack. You add a walk. A week later it has moved half a pound, then crept back up.

This is the point where most people decide the diet "stopped working," or that something is wrong with them.

There is a more useful question to ask: what if the plan stayed the same while your body changed?

The calorie number you started with is already out of date

A smaller body burns less energy, every day, just by existing. That is simple physics, and it is easy to forget when you are focused on the scale.

Most plans give you one calorie target on day one and never touch it again. It was calculated for the body you had at the start, not the one you have in week eight.

So the gap between what you eat and what you burn slowly shrinks. Nothing went wrong. The math moved underneath you.

The short version: your calorie target is a moving number. A plan that never updates it will eventually stall, no matter how disciplined you are.

Three more things that hide your progress

IMAGE: home kitchen, simple meal, notebook

1. Water. Salt, poor sleep, stress and hormones can move the scale a kilogram or more in either direction. You can lose fat for weeks while the number stays flat.

2. Small tracking gaps. A weekend dinner, a few drinks, snacks while cooking. Each seems minor. Together they can quietly cancel the deficit you thought you had.

3. Strict eating with no breaks. Hunger rises and willpower fades the longer you stay in a hard deficit. That is why so many people lose for two months and then regain. The plan asked for something nobody can keep doing.

What actually fixes it: recalculating as you go

The fix is not a trick. We call it the Recalculation Method, and it has three parts:

  • Recheck every two weeks. Your calorie and protein targets are recalculated for your current weight, so the deficit stays real.
  • Plan the plateau. Around month three the plan includes a maintenance week and a recheck. A planned pause keeps you going. An unplanned one usually ends the diet.
  • Build around what derails you. Evening cravings, stress eating, no time to cook, not knowing what to eat. The plan is set up around your one biggest obstacle, so you are not relying on motivation alone.

What a realistic six months looks like

IMAGE: person walking outdoors, relaxed

Steady fat loss is usually about 0.5 to 1 kg (1 to 2 lb) per week, depending on where you start. Anyone promising much faster is selling water loss or a plan you will not be able to keep.

Over six months that pace adds up to a real change. More important, it is a pace most people can actually sustain, which is what decides whether the weight stays off.

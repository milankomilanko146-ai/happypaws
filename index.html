How I Got Here

My name is Rachel. I'm 44. I work part-time and I have two teenagers at home.

My tension didn't start with an injury. No accident. No dramatic moment. It just slowly crept in — around the time my youngest started high school and life got busier and more stressful. I started waking up stiff. By mid-morning my shoulders were up near my ears. By evening my neck ached.

My GP said it was stress. She was probably right. She said to try yoga and take breaks from my desk.

I tried yoga. My shoulders were tense during downward dog.

Over the next four years I tried everything that seemed reasonable:

  • Remedial massage — I went fortnightly for over a year. It felt wonderful for about 48 hours. Then exactly the same tension was back.
  • Physiotherapy exercises — neck rolls, shoulder rotations, doorframe stretches. I did them every single morning. Some days they helped a little. They never fixed anything.
  • Heat packs every evening — helped me fall asleep. I still woke up stiff.
  • Anti-inflammatories — took them so regularly I started to worry about my stomach. They worked sometimes. The tension was still there when they wore off.
  • An expensive ergonomic chair — no change. The tension was there even on days I didn't sit at a desk.
  • Pilates for a year — I actually loved it. Great for my core. My shoulders were still tense for the whole class.

I want to say this clearly: I was not lazy. I was not a bad patient. I showed up. I did the work.

The tension just kept coming back. Every time. Without fail.

And not one person ever properly explained to me why.

The Conversation That Changed Everything

My friend Kate has been a physiotherapist for six years. We were having coffee and I was doing what I apparently always do — pressing my fingers into the knot under my right shoulder blade without even thinking about it.

She watched me do it and said: "How long has that been there?"

I said four years. She made a face.

"Have you ever heard of chronic muscle hypertonicity?" she asked.

I hadn't.

She put her coffee down and explained it like this:

When a muscle stays tense for a long enough period — not just weeks but months or years — it gradually resets its resting state. The tension stops being a temporary reaction to stress. It becomes the new default. The muscle, in a very real sense, forgets what relaxed feels like. It starts receiving signals that tell it "this is normal. Stay here."

At that point, she said, everything I had been trying was only ever going to give me temporary relief. Massage, heat, and stretching work on the surface — they reduce tension in the moment. But they cannot change the underlying signal the muscle is receiving. The second the treatment is over, the muscle returns to what it now considers its normal position: contracted and tense.

"It's like trying to flatten a piece of paper that's been rolled up for years," she said. "You can hold it flat while you press on it. The second you let go, it curls straight back."

Why Nothing I Tried Could Ever Last

Here is the simple version of what she explained:

Your muscles receive electrical signals from your nervous system telling them to contract or relax. When chronic tension sets in, the "stay contracted" signal becomes the new baseline. The muscle is essentially waiting for a stronger signal to override it.

Massage, heat, and stretching cannot send that stronger signal. They work on the tissue from the outside — but the tissue is receiving the wrong instruction at an electrical level, and nothing on the outside can change that instruction.

The only thing that can reach that level directly is an electrical signal strong enough to force the muscle through a full contraction-and-release cycle — essentially resetting the neuromuscular pattern, the way a restart resets a frozen computer.

This is called neuromuscular electrical stimulation (TENS/EMS). It has been used in clinical physiotherapy for decades. Most people have either never heard of it, or they've only encountered the cheap TENS pads at the pharmacy — which work on a completely different principle (they distract from pain at the surface level, rather than resetting the muscle pattern underneath).

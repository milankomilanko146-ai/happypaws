Inflammation vs. Tension: The Difference That Changes Everything

Inflammation is what happens after an acute injury — a sprained ankle, a torn muscle, surgery. Your immune system floods the area with inflammatory compounds as part of the healing response. In that context, anti-inflammatories are genuinely useful. They reduce swelling, protect the tissue, and make recovery more comfortable.

Chronic muscle pain — the kind that settles into your upper back after a long week, the persistent tension in your neck that never quite goes away, the tight hips that make you shuffle on Monday mornings — is different. It's not injury inflammation. It's mechanical tension: muscles that have been held in a shortened, contracted position for too long, with blood flow restricted and waste products accumulating in the tissue.

The key distinction: Ibuprofen blocks inflammatory signals. But mechanical muscle tension doesn't produce significant inflammatory signals — it produces a different kind of pain entirely, driven by oxygen restriction, fascial tightening, and neural hypersensitivity in the tissue. Anti-inflammatories address none of these mechanisms.

This is why the ibuprofen takes the edge off — it's still mildly analgesic — but never actually resolves the problem. You wake up the next morning and it's back. You take more. The cycle continues for years.

What's Really Going On Inside the Muscle

When a muscle is chronically tense, here's the sequence of events. The muscle fibres are partially contracted — not fully, but enough to restrict the blood vessels running through the tissue. Reduced blood flow means reduced oxygen delivery and reduced removal of metabolic waste products. Those waste products — lactic acid, adenosine, bradykinin — are pain signals. Your nervous system registers their accumulation and sends pain to your brain.

Meanwhile, the fascia — the connective tissue that surrounds and separates muscle groups — begins to stiffen around the contracted muscle. It becomes less hydrated, less pliable. Adhesions form between tissue layers that should slide freely. Those adhesions create the "knots" you can feel with your fingers — and the pulling sensation when you try to turn your head or straighten your back.

"Prescribing anti-inflammatories for chronic tension pain is like treating dehydration with a salt tablet. You're addressing the wrong mechanism entirely." — Dr. Priya Mehta, Musculoskeletal Physiotherapist, London

None of this is caused by inflammation. None of it is addressed by anti-inflammatory medication. The only thing that resolves mechanical tension at its source is mechanical intervention — something that physically forces blood into the restricted tissue, breaks up fascial adhesions, and resets the nervous system's tension response in the affected area.

Walk into any sports physio clinic and look at what's on the treatment table. Alongside hands-on manual therapy, you'll find percussion devices — tools that deliver rapid, targeted pulses into the muscle tissue.
